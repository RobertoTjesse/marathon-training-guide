01

Training: plans, volume & key workouts

A marathon block typically lasts 16–20 weeks, with beginners at the longer end. The foundation is consistent weekly mileage, one long run a week, and a small dose of faster running. Choose a plan that matches your experience and current base.

35–45 miles / week 4–5 run days Long run: 2–6 → 20 mi First or second marathon

For your first marathon (or a comeback). Enter the block with a base of roughly 15–20 miles per week. Your long run should build to at least two 18-milers before the taper, peaking around 20 miles. Keep most midweek runs easy and conversational.

45–60 miles / week 5 run days Long run: 5–10 → 20–22 mi A few marathons done

You've finished a few marathons and want to train more intentionally. Add a midweek medium-long run and one or two quality sessions (tempo or intervals) each week. Work up to a handful of 16–20-mile long runs, with 20–22 miles as the peak target.

55–70 miles / week 5–6 run days Long run: 10 → 20–23 mi Time-goal focused

For experienced runners chasing an aggressive finish time. Higher volume plus regular marathon-pace work in long runs, tempo runs and intervals. At least two 20-milers (and up to 22–23 miles) during peak training. Fast time goals may demand 70–80 miles per week.

Two rules of thumb. Your long run should make up roughly 30–40% of weekly mileage (up to half is acceptable if pacing and recovery are sound). And increase weekly mileage by no more than about 10% per week — with a cutback week (drop 20–25%) every 3–4 weeks.

The four key workouts

Foundation

The long run

One weekly long run builds cardiovascular and muscular endurance and teaches your body to burn fat. Run it at an easy, conversational pace — generally 35 seconds or more per mile slower than goal marathon pace. It's also where you rehearse fueling and hydration.

Endurance

Tempo / threshold

A sustained effort at a "comfortably hard" pace — roughly 20–30 seconds per mile faster than marathon pace. Builds the ability to clear lactate, raising the pace you can hold for 26.2 miles. Typical session: 4–5 miles at threshold inside a longer run.

Speed

Intervals

Repeats of shorter, faster efforts (e.g. 800 m–1 mile at 5K pace, or mile repeats 30 s/mile faster than race pace) with recovery jogs between. They lift aerobic capacity and running economy. One interval session a week is plenty during marathon training.

Specificity

Marathon-pace work

Segments at goal race pace — either as 3–10 × 1-mile repeats with short rest, 6 × 2K at marathon pace, or "marathon-pace long runs" where the last 6–12 miles of a long run are at goal pace. It builds confidence that you can hold race pace when tired.

Pacing & race strategy

The pacing strategy with the most data behind it is near-even pacing — or a very small negative split (second half slightly faster). The single most expensive mistake is starting too fast: time "banked" early is paid back with interest after mile 20.

Miles 1–3

Run 10–30 seconds per mile slower than goal pace. It will feel too easy — that's the taper talking, not fitness. If it feels easy, you're doing it right.

Miles 4–20

Settle into goal pace and hold a controlled, even effort. Don't chase time lost in start-line congestion all at once — spread it over several miles.

Final 10K

Start racing on feel. If you're strong, gradually wind the pace down — no giant surges. A controlled second half should land within 2–3% of the first.

Even effort, not even pace. On hills, run by effort: ease up going up, and use the downhill. In heat, start slower than planned and drink to your plan. Use average pace per mile, not the watch's flickering "instant pace."
03

Nutrition & hydration

Marathon fueling has three acts: carb-load before, fuel steadily during, and recover after. Practice all of it on long runs — never debut a fueling plan on race day.

Before

Carb-load

Load 1–3 days out, not one giant pasta dinner. Keep pre-race meals high-carb, low-fiber, low-fat. Eat 1–4 g of carbs per kg of bodyweight 2–4 hours before the start; sip 8–16 oz of water in the final 1–2 hours.

During

Fuel on the move

Aim for 30–90 g of carbs per hour (start lower, build tolerance), spaced every 30 minutes. Add 200–500 mg sodium and roughly 300–800 ml fluid per hour, guided by your sweat rate. Gels, chews and sports drinks together are easiest to tolerate.

After

Refuel & rehydrate

Eat carbs + protein within 30–60 minutes. Rehydrate over about six hours — roughly 16–24 oz of fluid per pound of bodyweight lost (weigh yourself before and after to learn your sweat rate).

Start early, don't react late. Begin fueling within the first 15–30 minutes — gut tolerance is usually best early. Set a watch alert every 30–50 minutes rather than waiting until you feel hungry or "hit the wall."
04

Gear & the race-day checklist

Rule number one: nothing new on race day. Every item you race in should be tested on at least one long run beforehand.

Shoes

  • Get a proper fit — arch, length, width and volume all matter.
  • A training cycle can run 300–500 miles, about one shoe's lifespan — plan on at least two pairs, or rotate a cushioned pair (easy/long runs) with a faster pair (speedwork).
  • Buy a fresh race pair and put 20–50 miles on them so they're broken in but still fresh.

Clothing & extras

  • Wicking, quick-dry technical fabrics — never cotton — with flat seams to limit chafing.
  • Technical socks, anti-chafe balm, and sun protection.
  • Hydration (vest, belt or handheld) and your race fuel, counted out for your projected finish time.
  • A GPS watch for pacing, plus recovery shoes and dry clothes in your post-race bag.
Pack list: race shoes · technical socks · race kit · fuel (gels/chews/drink) · hydration · anti-chafe · GPS watch · warm-up throwaway layers · sun protection · post-race clothes & recovery shoes.
05

Injury prevention & strength

Most running injuries are overuse injuries — the result of doing too much, too soon. The most common: runner's knee (patellofemoral pain), IT band syndrome, shin splints, Achilles tendinopathy, plantar fasciitis and stress fractures.

Build gradually

Increase weekly mileage by no more than 10%. Avoid sudden "spikes" in volume or intensity — muscles and tendons adapt to gradual load, not jumps.

Strength train twice

Two sessions a week (ideally 48 h apart) of lower-body, core and hip work — especially hip abductors and external rotators — measurably reduces injury risk.

Warm up & recover

5–10 minutes of dynamic movement (high knees, skips, lunges) before, and a light jog + static stretching after. Schedule genuine rest days every week.

Know the difference. General soreness after a run is normal. But pain that worsens during activity, persists after a couple of days' rest, or comes with limping or swelling is a signal to back off and, if needed, see a professional — don't push through it.

Taper & race week

A taper is a planned, progressive cut in volume over the final ~3 weeks (19–22 days) that lets your body absorb training and arrive fresh. You keep intensity and frequency — you cut volume.

WhenMileage (vs. peak week)Long runIntensity
3 weeks out~75–80%Last long run, 16–18 miDrop speedwork; easy + some race pace
2 weeks out~50–65%10–12 miHalf the intervals, keep the pace
Race week~20–30%Short & easy (6–8 mi total)2–3 easy runs + a few strides

Most runs in the taper are done 90 seconds to 2 minutes per mile slower than goal pace. The day before, an optional 20–30 minute easy jog with a few short pickups; two days before, take a full rest day.

07

Post-race recovery

Recovery isn't one-size-fits-all, but there's a reliable shape: rest, then a gradual "reverse taper." Take at least 3–7 days fully off from running, then ease back over 3–4 weeks.

Week 1

Rest plus light, low-impact cross-training (walking, cycling, swimming, yoga). If all feels well, a single easy 30-minute run later in the week.

Week 2

Return to normal running frequency but keep it easy and shorter (30–60 min). No speedwork or racing for 2–3 weeks.

Week 3

Step up distance and intensity slightly if your body feels good. First long run back is ~6 miles, then 8–10 the following weekend.

Week 4

Return to your pre-marathon schedule once you feel ready. Some experts suggest ~1 day of recovery per mile raced (≈26 days) before full training.

You're ready when… resting heart rate returns to your pre-marathon baseline, HRV stabilizes, and muscle soreness is gone. Full recovery commonly takes 2–4 weeks, sometimes a month or more.
